National Bank of Ethiopia connects to PAPSS payments network

FinTech Futures · 19 Oct 2026

The National Bank of Ethiopia (NBE) has joined the Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS) network.

PAPSS was launched in 2022 by Egypt-based Afreximbank with the purpose of streamlining cross-border payments between countries across Africa. Since its establishment, PAPSS has now grown to connect 29 African countries, enabling more than 190 commercial banks and fintechs to transact across the continent.

Commenting on the milestone, Dr Eyob Tekalign, governor of the National Bank of Ethiopia, says: "Ethiopia’s integration into the PAPSS network represents an important step in our continued efforts to modernise our financial system and deepen our economic connections with the rest of Africa. It creates new opportunities for Ethiopian financial institutions and businesses to participate more actively in cross-border payments and intra-African trade."

Senegal bonds fall after Sonko opposes budget bills

CNBC Africa · 21 Oct 2026

DAKAR/JOHANNESBURG (Reuters) – Senegal’s international bonds fell sharply on Tuesday after National Assembly President Ousmane Sonko said lawmakers would not back budget legislation, raising fresh doubts over how quickly the government can push ahead with its debt rework.

All of Senegal’s euro- and dollar-denominated bond maturities fell more than a cent on the day, with longer-dated debt suffering the sharpest losses. The 2037 bond dropped 2.166 cents to bid at 48.931 cents on the euro, Tradeweb data showed.

Sonko remains one of Senegal’s most powerful political figures despite being fired as prime minister by President Bassirou Diomaye Faye in May. He leads the ruling PASTEF party, which controls 130 of parliament’s 165 seats, and was elected speaker with the backing of 132 lawmakers, giving him significant influence over Faye’s legislative agenda.

PP110: Negotiating the state: Political participation in post-conflict Côte d’Ivoire

Afrobarometer · 22 Oct 2026

Côte d’Ivoire has experienced recurring political instability since the death of President Félix  Houphouët-Boigny in 1993. The political battles that followed exposed longstanding tensions over citizenship and Ivoirité , deepening regional, ethnic, and political divisions. These tensions resulted in a 1999 military coup, election-related violence in 2000, a 2002 rebellion that divided the country, and the disputed 2010 presidential election, which triggered a violent post-election conflict that claimed more than 3,000 lives (Akindès, 2004; Cheeseman, 2015;  Wells & Dufka, 2011).

Political participation provides an important lens through which to understand democratic consolidation in post-conflict societies. It includes institutionalised forms of engagement, such as contacting elected representatives, as well as non-institutionalised activities such as protest (Theocharis & van Deth, 2017; van Deth, 2021). In fragile political environments,  participation is often shaped by institutional trust, citizens’ experiences of economic hardship,  cognitive engagement such as access to information and political discussion among friends and family, and perceptions of government responsiveness (Bratton & van de Walle, 1997;  Guseva, 2024; Verba, Schlozman, & Brady, 1995). In many African contexts, citizens may contact political leaders not only to seek assistance, but also to demand recognition and inclusion in political decision making. Protest, meanwhile, can provide a channel through which citizens express grievances and demand accountability (Klaus, Paller, & Wilfahrt, 2023;  Muller & Seligson, 1987; Harris & Hern, 2019).

Much of the political-science literature on Côte d’Ivoire has focused on citizenship, armed conflict, electoral violence, civil-military relations, and ethnic politics (Akindès, 2004; Schiel,  Faulkner, & Powell, 2017). While these studies have deepened understanding of the country’s political trajectory, less attention has been paid to how ordinary citizens engage with governance institutions in the post-conflict period. This paper addresses that gap by examining factors associated with two forms of political participation: contacting elected representatives and participating in demonstrations or protest marches.

How El Niño is driving hunger and drought across Central America

UN World Food Programme · 22 Oct 2026

During my travels across Central America in recent months, I have witnessed how drought driven by El Niño has evolved from a distant threat to reality and, today, an emergency.

The farmers of a small agricultural cooperative in the northern community of San Diego, Nicaragua, whom I met at the end of April, had no doubts: rainfall prospects were bleak, even by the standards of Central America's Dry Corridor. Rising fertilizer prices, up by nearly 50 percent since the beginning of the year, darkened the outlook.

Those predictions were soon confirmed. By July, in Jalapa, Guatemala, farmers guided me through plots where crops had barely reached half their normal height. And by the time I arrived in Honduras in early September, communities and authorities were already sounding the alarm: dry wells and rivers, failed harvests and dead livestock.
