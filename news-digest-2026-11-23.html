Agrifood & Climate

Benin: Food Security Outlook - Amid ongoing harvests, insecurity in northern border areas constrains food access, October 2026 - May 2027

ReliefWeb · 23 Nov 2026

Despite slight improvements from the previous IPC analysis, acute food insecurity in Lebanon remains widespread across all population groups and is particularly severe among refugees and recently displaced Lebanese populations. Household-level evidence continues to show severe pressures on livelihoods, purchasing power and coping capacity, while livelihoods, purchasing power and household resilience remain severely constrained. The improvement compared to the previous analysis also reflects a reduction in the overall population of Syrian refugees. Between September 2026 and February 2027, approximately 1.05 million people, representing 21 percent of the analysed population, are experiencing IPC Phase 3 or above (Crisis or worse), including 52,000 people in Emergency (IPC Phase 4) and approximately 997,000 people in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis).

The situation is driven by the cumulative impacts of two consecutive conflicts in less than two years, compounded by a fragile economic environment and rising living costs. While the October 2025 IPC analysis reflected the impact of the 2023–2024 conflict and subsequent ceasefire, the current analysis assesses food security outcomes following a second major conflict escalation that began in March 2026. Although the intensity of the conflict has decreased since the June 2026 ceasefire agreement, the security situation remains fragile, with hostilities continuing in southern Lebanon despite ongoing diplomatic efforts to secure a more durable ceasefire. This prolonged insecurity has further undermined households’ capacity to recover from previous losses, displacement, livelihood disruptions, and asset depletion.

Food insecurity in Lebanon is increasingly shaped by deteriorating livelihoods and constrained economic access to food. Although markets remain functional and food is generally available across most areas of the country, households face significant constraints in accessing it due to weak income opportunities and reduced purchasing power. The combined effects of conflict-related livelihood losses, slow reconstruction, elevated food and fuel prices, and rising transportation and energy costs continue to erode households’ economic access to food. As food prices remain elevated and incomes fail to recover at the same pace as living costs, many households continue to rely on debt, consumption-based and livelihood coping strategies, and humanitarian food security assistance (HFSA) to meet their basic food needs. The cumulative impact of these drivers has reduced households’ ability to absorb further shocks, particularly among vulnerable Lebanese households, refugees, agricultural communities, displaced populations, and residents of conflict-affected areas.

Central Sahel, Burkina Faso

Global Centre for the Responsibility to Protect · 16 Nov 2026

Populations in the Central Sahel – Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger – are enduring armed conflict and inter-communal violence amidst an entrenched and expanding insurgency driven by armed Islamist groups affiliated with al-Qaeda (JNIM) and the so-called Islamic State Sahel Province (ISSP). Violence has also taken place between rival ethnic militias and community-based self-defense groups resulting in reprisal attacks and countless abuses. There have been thousands of conflict-related civilian deaths, including 2,640 recorded by the UN between January and March 2026.

Armed Islamist groups perpetrate recurrent abuses and attacks against civilians. These groups systematically use sieges, threats, kidnappings, improvised explosive devices, drones and landmines as deliberate tactics of war as they seek to control supply routes and increase areas of influence. They have increasingly consolidated control along major transport corridors, isolating communities through blockades, disrupting markets and restricting civilian movement, including in urban areas. In besieged areas, armed Islamist groups are blocking humanitarian aid to civilians and causing starvation, imposing forced taxation and strategically destroying and looting civilian objects, including places of worship, health centers, food reserves and water services, among other violations of International Humanitarian Law (IHL). They have also enforced their own interpretation of Sharia law in areas under their control, imposing severe gender discriminatory rules. Insurgents also routinely perpetrate grave violations against children and target secular state education, burning schools and threatening, abducting or killing teachers.

Counterterrorism operations have often led to human rights violations in all three states, with security forces in Mali and Burkina Faso implicated in likely war crimes and crimes against humanity. The Malian Armed Forces (FAMa) and Russian state-controlled Africa Corps (formerly the Wagner Group) have perpetrated possible war crimes and crimes against humanity, including summary executions, indiscriminate airstrikes, rape and sexual violence, pillaging and torture against civilians, among other abuses. State-sponsored militias in Burkina Faso, notably the Volunteers for the Defense of the Homeland (VDP), have also been implicated in grave crimes along ethnic lines. According to Human Rights Watch, the ethnic targeting of Fulani civilians by the Burkinabè military and VDPs through killings, enforced disappearances and looting, which resulted in the mass displacement of entire communities, amounts to ethnic cleansing.
